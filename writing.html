Some fragments

It’s strange to think that I spent those six weeks hearing about your life, drawing a mental picture of you, filling in the details, one by one by one, like one of the old masters, each night shading a hand or enhancing an eyebrow or adjusting the lips, here a joy and there a grief, and by the end of it I had a beautiful portrait—and as time passed the portrait, beautiful at any distance, fell further and further back, the details recessing into obscurity; but I knew the silhouette so well, knew it so well I could intuit any part of its source when given a single slit, a pixel—and that, I think, is the nature of a friendship that approaches a singularity so quickly and survives the ensuing separation, temporal, spatial, biographical.

(From One decade of Siva, written October 2026.)

Siva I’ve tied to the climbing gym, his apartment, the observatory atop the Harvard Science Center—you, though, you own the entirety of Back Bay. You own Newbury Street in particular: Hey Tea and its tiny bench opposite the register, where many times we’ve idled and blathered; Trident Booksellers, where we’ve stopped two or three times to buy books, and twenty or thirty for the free public restroom; countless clothing stores, where you find your fits and I hold our drinks, or sit on the floor and work on my homework, or nod approvingly, cluelessly, when you bring me what you’re planning to purchase. You own the Jaho in Cambridge. You own the Ma La Tang in Allston. You own Tatte, every Tatte in the world except the one across the street from my apartment, which we’ve never visited.

I think about Holden. I sometimes wonder whether we are friends, but I know we are collaborators. Friendship and collaboration are different paths to understanding, which I think of as the crown jewel of the trio. I can count the number of people I feel I understand on one hand. I see how Carissa gives such obvious, tangible love and care to her friends and family, how she wants so desperately to pursue literature and philosophy despite her other obligations, so when she asks me to describe her in three words, after a half-joking flighty, I tell her wholehearted and yearning. love you!!!, she texts. u get me. I don’t see Holden, but I’ve studied and engaged with his work. He is principled. He doesn’t play his pieces because he believes his recordings would become the reference recordings, which would limit future interpreters’ imaginations. He refuses to talk about the emotions his music is to evoke because he thinks it speaks for itself; he talks about it as if it were an intellectual exercise—with an air of detachment—despite how deeply it is to be felt. He prefers sound to language.

I understand Holden, I think.

(From Displacement from the self, written June 2026.)

To attempt to make sense of these contradictions, this overflow of information, is futile, and simultaneously, the only thing we can do and the only thing worth doing. Such is the labor of literature and of life. For to those who grow old, sense-making is a slow, arduous, yet rewarding process—a few meaningless encounters bloom, decades later, into a romance; a paragraph from a book read as a teenager resurfaces as one enters their fifties; the surely incompatible wisdoms of one’s father and mother unify themselves in adulthood; the tendrils of life, long as they are, have the chance to organize themselves neatly, to weave themselves into tapestries that proclaim to the world, this is who I am.

A premature, self-inflicted death, then, forces a sudden reckoning. Those meaningless encounters will never bloom. That paragraph will never resurface. One’s parents will be locked in eternal conflict. The tendrils of life, cut short, are interpreted not as curving here and there, seemingly at random, so that they might eventually come together in some beautiful form, but rather as pointing—all of them, in some way or another, forever and ever—toward suicide.

(From How to enjoy a swimming pool, written December 2025.)

The first movement features a delicate, lyrical theme, patterned and predictable in a way that soothes and consoles: everything will be okay. The theme then crystallizes. The harmony, which once swayed gently, back and forth each beat or two, around the home key and in its orbit, finds itself now solid, hard-edged, far from home—diminished. The pianist pauses. The listener is led to luxuriate in this sudden stagnancy, which signals the start of an interlude. Slow, regal, chordal, the interlude soon expands into a series of keyboard-spanning arpeggios and, after a steep descending scale, contracts upward to a single note that heralds the return of the first theme. This back-and-forth of theme and interlude, both sweet but not sickly so, characterizes the first movement, which closes with a major chord under a fermata, an instruction for the pianist to hold tight, to prolong the comforting harmony—

(From Against vibes, written January 2026.)

[Having] talked to many young people fueled by the sense that certain technology is just interesting, just fascinating, “technically sweet,” the sort of work that engrosses you for hours and years on end, I have an instinctive wariness of the attitude I associate with this word—the attitude that as technologists, we need only concern ourselves with the technical specifics, and someone else will handle the rest. It is interesting, indeed, that safety regulations are written in blood, and it is interesting that we who develop technology must grapple with the idea that we can and often will have blood on our hands.

(From I really dislike Naomi Bashkansky’s blog post “Why I’m leaving OpenAI to build telepathy.”, written August 2026.)

Nocturne opens monochromatically—the first phrase, built entirely from the white keys, comprises notes from a single Lydian scale.

In capturing beauty, the snapshot-aesthetic of dawn—kaleidoscopic, the sun bisected by the horizon—neglects its essence. Beauty is not from moments but from processes. What is a gorgeous harmonic resolution without the preceding tension? So Nocturne is dawn, and in reality dawn begins not with crimson or magenta, but with deep blue.

(From Nocturne, written April 2025.)
